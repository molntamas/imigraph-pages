Map (マップ) is Conway's Game of Life, in Japanese ライフゲーム: a cell is born with exactly 3 alive neighbours, stays alive with 2 or 3, and dies otherwise. There are only two kinds of cells, alive and dead; a dead cell is empty ground. A finger or the mouse sets and unsets cells; two fingers or the wheel zoom and move.

The colours of alive cells tell how long a cell has been alive, so that you see at a glance what is new and what stays:

  • just born: alive for the first generation (also a cell you have just set)
  • young: alive since the generation before
  • old: alive for 12 generations or more

In between, the colour passes step by step from young to old (close up, where cells are faces, the face changes at 12). So a block, which never changes, is soon all old; a glider and everything that blinks keep showing new and young cells; and where something is happening, the colours are mixed. The lights of the ring have the same colours. One look leaves the ages out: in Kuroi (Look, further up in these settings) there is black and white and nothing else, and an alive cell is white however old it is.

Donut (ドーナツ) is the same world with its edges joined. The map wraps round: a glider that leaves on the right comes back on the left, one that leaves at the bottom comes back at the top. On the flat map you have to remember that; the donut shows it. Glue the left and right edges of the map together and you get a tube; bend the tube until its two ends meet and you get a ring. The columns of the map go round the ring, the rows round its tube. Nothing about the game changes: every cell keeps its eight neighbours, and the rule is the same. Only the sizes are not true, as on a globe: cells on the inner side of the ring sit closer together than those on the outer side, though on the map they are all the same.

The donut as a hypergraph. The same world can be written as a hypergraph, the kind of structure the Wolfram Physics Project builds worlds from: nothing but nodes and relations between them. Every cell is a node: a faint grey rock while it is dead, a light while it is alive. What is further away from you is fainter. Each node carries 20 relations in all: one that names it with its eight neighbours, nine that put those neighbours in an order, one that says alive or dead, one that says it is ready, and eight lines to its neighbours. A hypergraph knows no places, only which nodes are related. Place the nodes by those relations alone, and because the world wraps round they close into a ring: the same donut as the glued map. Drag to turn the ring any way you like, also to see it from the top (Ring, further up in these settings, has ready views); tap a node to set or unset its cell.

The lines of the ring. The thin dark lines are the relations that never change, those that say which cells are neighbours. The coloured lines are life signals: in the Wolfram model every generation begins with each node sending its state along its eight lines, and an alive node sends "alive". A coloured line is one that carries "alive": it has the colour of the node that sends, strong at that node and fading towards a dead neighbour; between two alive nodes it is strong all the way. A dead node that exactly three coloured lines reach is born in the next generation. Links and signals are drawn for worlds of up to 128 x 128 cells; the switch Life signals (Ring, in these settings) turns the colours off.

Organism (いきもの) shows one organism close up, in a window on the grid of the ring, laid flat. An organism is a group of alive cells whose signals meet (they are at most two cells apart): a glider, a block, a blinker. The window shows as much of the grid round it as the screen has room for, so a larger screen shows more. Every cell is a node, the same distance from node to node everywhere, so an organism has the shape it has on the map. The thin dark lines and the coloured life signals are those of the ring; the organism shown has the strong lines, others nearby have fainter ones. The picture stands still: it moves only when the organism comes near the edge of the window, and then far enough ahead that it has the whole window to cross.

The two arrow buttons go to the organism before and to the next one (they are numbered from the top of the map down), and so does a tap on the picture; on the small map, which shows the whole world and a frame round the window, tap an organism to look at it. The world plays slowly on this screen, one generation a second at most. A large organism is drawn small: first the faces become blocks, then the lines are left out.

Next, the button at the bottom left of that screen, changes the picture into a forecast of the coming generation. Every node then carries a number: how many of its eight neighbours are alive, its life support. And colours say what comes:

  • a filled light tile is alive and stays alive: its number is 2 or 3
  • a filled red tile is alive and dies: its number is below 2 or above 3
  • a hollow tile is a dead cell that is born: its number is exactly 3
  • a grey number or a rock is a dead cell that stays dead

The legend beside the button counts them. Press Next again for the organism as it is. What the window shows of the hypergraph: of every node the relation that says alive or dead and the one that names its eight neighbours, and the eight lines of every alive node. Left out are each node's round, its ready mark, and the lines of the dead nodes.

Cell (セル) looks into one cell of that organism, as through a magnifying glass. On the donut and in the window of an organism a cell is one node. Here that node is opened up. This is what the inside of a Game of Life cell could look like when Life is played by a more general kind of system: one that knows nothing of cells or of Life, only of nodes, of arrows between them, and of rules that rewrite small pieces of such a graph (a Wolfram model; an arrow is the simplest relation a hypergraph can have, one between two nodes).

In such a graph a cell is not one node any more. It is a small graph of its own: a ring of eight nodes, its doors, one for each neighbour. The soft pad under a ring shows which nodes are one cell; the cell you look at has the frame, its eight neighbours lie round it.

  • An arrow joins two nodes and points from the first to the second. The eight arrows from door to door are the rim of a ring.
  • Between the doors that face each other in two neighbouring cells run two arrows, one each way: the lines along which the two cells tell each other their state.
  • Every cell has four more arrows, its cords. In a dead cell they rest along the rim: grey, four sides of the ring are double. In an alive cell they are stretched across the ring, each to the door opposite, in the colour of the cell's age. Being born and dying make and remove nothing: four arrows swing across, or back.
  • A coloured line leaves an alive cell: it will carry "alive" when the next generation begins. Count the coloured lines that reach a cell: exactly three make a dead cell alive.

A knot. A ring with its four cords across cannot be drawn on paper without two arrows crossing, however the nodes are moved (it holds the graph mathematicians call K3,3). A dead ring can. The Wolfram Physics Project pictures particles as such knots in a graph that is flat everywhere else: rules that never undo a knot would carry it along, like a particle. Life is not such a world. Its cells are born and die, so here the knot is only the mark of life, tied and undone by the rules; what travels in Life is a pattern like the glider, made anew from other cells in every generation. And why is a dead cell not simply an empty ring? A rewriting rule can only ask for something that is there, never for something missing, so "dead" needs a mark of its own: the same four cords, resting.

A cell is 8 nodes and 20 arrows: 8 of the rim, 4 cords, 8 lines out. 17 rewriting rules play Life on this graph, in the same three steps as on the hypergraph of the donut: a cell sends its state along its eight lines, reads the eight messages that come in and counts the alive ones, and then lays its cords. Tests hold that these rules give Life, cell for cell, and that between two generations the graph is exactly what this screen draws. The picture shows every arrow between the 72 nodes of the nine cells, 148 of them; left out are the lines to cells further away.

Where a node lies means nothing in a graph. The picture places the nodes by forces, the way the Wolfram project draws its graphs: every arrow pulls its two nodes together, all nodes push each other away. That nine cells lie three by three comes out of the arrows alone. Cell, further up in these settings, also has a tidy placing: every ring a regular octagon. Either way the picture stands still; only cords and colours change.

The lines at the bottom left say where the cell lies on the map, what it is, how many of its neighbours are alive, and what it is next. Tap the ring of a neighbour to go there, or a cell on the map of the organism at the top right (the frame there shows the nine cells of the picture). Tap an arrow to hear which one it is. The button under the map says what the picture does when the world moves on: Stay near keeps the cell while the organism is at it and moves on with the organism when it has left (a blinker's cell is watched as it blinks, a glider is followed); Stay on life moves to the nearest alive cell as soon as the cell dies, a newly born one first; Stay put never moves.

How the screens belong together: a Wolfram model of 17 rewriting rules plays Life on the hypergraph of the donut, cell for cell, and a second one, also of 17 rules, on the graph of arrows of the fourth screen. This game turns that round. It computes with the ordinary rule, which is far faster, and shows what the rules would hold at that moment. Tests hold them equal, relation for relation and arrow for arrow, between every two generations.
