Map (マップ) is Conway's Game of Life, in Japanese ライフゲーム: a cell is born with exactly 3 alive neighbours, stays alive with 2 or 3, and dies otherwise. There are only two kinds of cells, alive and dead; a dead cell is empty ground. A finger or the mouse sets and unsets cells; two fingers or the wheel zoom and move.

The colours of alive cells tell how long a cell has been alive, so that you see at a glance what is new and what stays:

  • just born: alive for the first generation (also a cell you have just set)
  • young: alive since the generation before
  • old: alive for 12 generations or more

In between, the colour passes step by step from young to old (close up, where cells are faces, the face changes at 12). So a block, which never changes, is soon all old; a glider and everything that blinks keep showing new and young cells; and where something is happening, the colours are mixed. The lights of the ring have the same colours.

Donut (ドーナツ) shows the same world as a hypergraph, the kind of structure the Wolfram Physics Project builds worlds from: nothing but nodes and relations between them. Every cell is a node: a faint grey rock while it is dead, a light while it is alive. What is further away from you is fainter. Because the world wraps round, the nodes close into a ring. Each node carries 20 relations in all: one that names it with its eight neighbours, nine that put those neighbours in an order, one that says alive or dead, one that says it is ready, and eight lines to its neighbours. Drag to turn the ring any way you like, also to see it from the top (Ring, further up in these settings, has ready views); tap a node to set or unset its cell.

The lines of the ring. The thin dark lines are the relations that never change, those that say which cells are neighbours. The coloured lines are life signals: in the Wolfram model every generation begins with each node sending its state along its eight lines, and an alive node sends "alive". A coloured line is one that carries "alive": it has the colour of the node that sends, strong at that node and fading towards a dead neighbour; between two alive nodes it is strong all the way. A dead node that exactly three coloured lines reach is born in the next generation. Links and signals are drawn for worlds of up to 128 x 128 cells; the switch Life signals (Ring, in these settings) turns the colours off.

Organism (いきもの) shows one organism the way the Wolfram Physics Project draws a hypergraph. An organism is a group of alive cells whose signals meet (they are at most two cells apart), with the dead cells around it: a glider, a block, a blinker. The nodes are placed by forces: what is linked pulls together, everything pushes apart. Where a node lies means nothing, so an organism does not look like its shape on the map, and it reshapes itself as it lives.

  • a face is an alive cell, in the colour of its age; the ring round it is its mark "alive"
  • a small hollow square is a dead cell around
  • a filled square is a cell that is born in the coming generation: exactly three arrows reach it
  • a dotted ring is a cell that dies in the coming generation: fewer than two arrows reach it, or more than three

The arrows are the eight lines out of an alive cell, along which it tells its neighbours that it is alive; the faint shape over a face and its eight neighbours is its neighbourhood. This is a cut of the organism's hypergraph: ten relations of every alive cell, and the mark of every cell around. Left out are each cell's round, its ready mark, the loops inside the marks and lines, and what the dead cells hold: all of it drawn is a tangle nobody can read. Tap the picture for the next organism; the small map in the corner shows where it is. An organism of more than 80 nodes is not drawn.

How the screens belong together: a Wolfram model of 17 rewriting rules plays Life on exactly this hypergraph, cell for cell. This game turns that round. It computes with the ordinary rule, which is far faster, and shows the hypergraph that the 17 rules would hold at that moment. Tests hold the two equal, relation for relation, between every two generations.
