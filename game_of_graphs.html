The tab "3D hypergraph". On the grid, every node is drawn where its cell is, which is a help for the eye and no part of the model: a hypergraph has no places, only relations. The second tab drops the grid. The place of every node is computed from one thing only, which nodes are related, so that related nodes lie near each other. The result is a ring (a doughnut): the world wraps round from left to right and from top to bottom, so it has no border anywhere, and a surface that closes on itself in two directions is a ring. Everything is drawn with the same marks as on the grid, listed above: black and hollow circles, grey lines between neighbours, blue and grey dots for messages, the orange reader, the count. What lies at the back of the ring is drawn fainter. Drag to turn the ring, zoom with the mouse wheel or two fingers.

Why the black dots seem to move. Nothing travels across the picture. In every generation the rules take the alive mark away from some nodes and put one on others, exactly where the Game of Life on the left lets cells die and be born. A glider "moves" on the right the same way it does on the left: as a pattern of marks, not as a thing.

Why you may see only black dots. Messages, readers and counts exist only while a generation is being worked out, in the steps in between. Played generation by generation, the picture shows only the results. Press 1 step a few times, or tick play step by step: after the first step every line carries a message, then the readers go round and the dots are taken away one by one, and with the tenth step the black marks change.

How it works

A rule of this kind can only look at a relation by taking it, so two neighbours cannot both read the mark of the cell between them in one step. A cell therefore sends a copy of its state to each of its eight neighbours. Then its reader goes once round the neighbours, takes one message at each and counts the "alive" ones. Back home, the count decides: 3 means alive, 2 means stay as you are, anything else means dead. That is one step for sending, eight for reading and one for deciding: ten steps, one generation.

Dead cells send a message too, because a rule cannot see that nothing came. And a cell only sends when all its eight lines are empty, so no message is ever overtaken. The whole model is the list of rules below and the start hypergraph; the rewriting is done by the engine of the experiment wolfram-physics, unchanged. Where a node is drawn (at its cell) is only a help for the eye and no part of the model.

How a rule is chosen, and why the result is the Game of Life

Nobody chooses. There is no program beside the rules that says "now this cell, now that rule". One step of the engine is always the same:

  1. It searches the whole hypergraph for every place where the left side of any of the 17 rules fits: where relations of the right sizes exist and share their nodes the way the rule says.
  2. It applies all of these at once. The relations of the left side are taken away, those of the right side are put in.
  3. If two fits want the same relation, only one of them is applied, picked by a fixed order (the "standard order" of the Wolfram model).

The third point never comes up in this model. It was built so that two changes never want the same relation: a rule used for cell c takes only relations of c itself and the one message that waits for c.

Which rule fits is decided by what the cell holds. At every moment a cell is in one of three situations, and in each of them exactly one of the 17 rules fits, in exactly one way:

the cell hasthe rule that fitswhat picks it among its family
a "ready" mark, and its eight lines are empty one of the 2 send rules its state mark: {c} (alive) or {c,c,c,c,c} (dead)
a reader that is not back home yet one of the 6 read rules the message that waits on the line from the next neighbour of the round (dead or alive), and for an alive one the count the cell holds (0, 1, 2, 3 or "4 or more")
a reader that is back home one of the 9 decide rules the count, and for every count but 2 the state mark

A rule can tell these things apart because every kind of relation has a size or a shape of its own (the table under "What the right picture shows" lists them), and a left side only fits relations of exactly its sizes and shapes. So in every step every cell makes exactly one change: in a world of 12 × 12 cells a step is 144 changes and a generation 1,440. The line under the right picture counts them.

Why ten such steps are one generation of Life.

  1. Step 1, send. Every cell puts a copy of its state on each of its eight lines. All these copies are of the same generation, because no cell has changed its state yet.
  2. Steps 2 to 9, read. The reader goes once round and takes one message per step. An "alive" message raises the count, a "dead" one leaves it. After eight steps the count is the number of alive neighbours. Everything above 3 is kept as "4 or more", because Life does not ask further.
  3. Step 10, decide. The nine decide rules are the rule of Life, written out case by case: count 3 makes the cell alive, count 2 leaves it as it is, every other count makes it dead.

The Game of Life asks that all cells change at once, each looking at the old states of its neighbours. Here that holds because states change only in step 10, and by then every cell has counted from messages that were written in step 1. Counting neighbours and applying "3 alive, 2 stays, else dead" is all that the ordinary program on the left does, so both sides must agree after every tenth step. The page does not rely on this argument: it compares the two worlds cell by cell after every generation and says so under the pictures.

What if the changes came in another order? The engine can also apply one fitting change at a time, picked at random. Then the cells get out of step with each other, but every cell still goes through the states of Life. The reason: a cell sends only when its eight lines are empty, and its reader takes exactly one message from every neighbour per round, so messages of two generations cannot be mixed up. This was tested for the experiment (in random order, and with a second, independent engine), not proved. This page always uses the standard order, in which all cells stay in step.

Why the model looks like this, and who did it before

A cell is one node. Alive or dead, a cell is the same single node for the whole game: a world of 12 × 12 cells has 144 nodes at every moment. "Alive" is one relation on that node, {c}, and "dead" is another, {c,c,c,c,c}; nothing else differs between the two. Between generations 20 relations start with a cell's node, and 52 name it once those of its eight neighbours are counted too.

What is forced. A Wolfram model has only bare nodes, relations, and rules that use up the relations they look at. Each thing it cannot do forces one part of the model:

a Wolfram model cannotso this model has
write a value on a node "alive" as a relation of its own, {c}
ask whether something is missing "dead" as a relation too, and messages from dead cells as well. Otherwise "my neighbour is dead" would look the same as "my neighbour has not sent yet"
let two changes read the same relation in one step a copy of the state for each of the eight neighbours: the messages
rely on a common clock a reader for every cell. Back home means "I have read all eight"

What was simply chosen. Which size of relation means what (any sizes would do, as long as every kind can be told from every other; telling kinds apart by size follows the Wolfram project's own model of the automaton "rule 30", where a black cell is a loop of size 6). One node per cell. The order in which the reader goes round. That the rule of Life sits in the rewriting rules (with the table of Life in the start graph, 3 rules would do instead of 17). That the world wraps round. Nobody claims this is the smallest model, and Life does not emerge here: the grid is in the start graph and the rules were written for the job.

Who did it before. In five web searches (not a full survey) the complete Game of Life as a Wolfram model was not found. The nearest is a personal project page, Game of Life on Hypergraphs, which by its search summary ran only a cut-down version with two neighbours. The Wolfram project itself shows a one-dimensional automaton and a Turing machine. In other kinds of graph rewriting, Life exists several times, for example in Parallel Graph Rewriting with Overlapping Rules, A Set-Theoretic Framework for Parallel Graph Rewriting and the Graph Grammar Library (known here from search summaries, not read). Those systems may write a value on a node and let many changes read the same thing at once, and then Life is one to seven rules and one step per generation. The 17 rules, the messages and the 10 steps are the price of doing without that.

The games: which numbers of neighbours count

There is no single Game of Life in three dimensions. Conway's numbers (born with 3, survives with 2 or 3) make almost every world die out or fill up when a cell has 26 neighbours, so a game says anew with how many alive neighbours a dead cell is born and an alive cell survives, written B6/S567 for "born with 6, survives with 5, 6 or 7". Carter Bays searched for the games that deserve the name (1987 and 1991) and proposed two, Life 5766 and Life 4555.

    The patterns are flat patterns of Conway's game, laid twice on top of each other. In Life 5766 such a slab plays the flat game: a cell of the slab counts twice its flat neighbours plus its twin, and "survives with 5 to 7, born with 6" then works out as "survives with 2 or 3, born with 3". The glider moves one cell in two directions every four generations. In the other two games the same slabs do something else; try them, or Random.

    How the model differs from the flat one

    It does not differ in kind: the same relations, the same three families of rules, only with 26 neighbours instead of 8, and with the numbers of the chosen game in the decide rules. A cell sends its state to its 26 neighbours (1 step), its reader takes the 26 messages one by one and counts the alive ones (26 steps), and the count decides (1 step). The same piece of code writes the rules for both: told "two dimensions, born with 3, survives with 2 or 3", it gives the 17 rules of the flat game, text for text (a test of the experiment holds that).

    A step costs more too: in every step the engine asks every rule where it fits, and the largest rule (send) has 29 relations on its left side. That is why a generation of a box of 12 × 12 × 12 cells takes about two seconds in the browser, where a flat world of 32 × 32 cells takes about a tenth of a second. The model was held against the ordinary program for every case of the rule (every state with every number of alive neighbours), in random order of the changes and with a second, independent engine; not proved, tested.
