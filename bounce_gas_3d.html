What a block automaton in three dimensions is

A block automaton (technical name: a cellular automaton with the Margolus neighbourhood, after Norman Margolus) does not look at every cell and its neighbours, as Conway's Game of Life does. It cuts the world into blocks and replaces each block as a whole. In three dimensions a block is a little cube of 2 × 2 × 2 cells. Each of its eight cells is empty or holds a particle, so a block can be in 256 states, and the whole rule is a table of 256 entries.

If the cut stayed the same, nothing could ever leave its block. So the cut moves: on even steps the blocks start at the first corner of the box, on odd steps one cell further in all three directions (the two cuts, or phases). Every block of an odd step holds one cell of each of eight blocks of the even step before, and that is how things travel. At the far sides the blocks of odd steps take their other cells from the near sides: the world wraps round in every direction. The number of cells along each side is even.

The rule: Bounce Gas 3D, this project's own

A search of the literature found the idea (blocks of 2 × 2 × 2 cells whose cut moves by one cell in every direction) but no named gas made with it; the flat Bounce Gas II (a rule by Tim Tyler) has no 3D version that could be found. So the rule here was designed for this page, in the spirit of Bounce Gas II, and measured (in the research bounce-gas-3d of the project). It says, for each block:

  • Fly: every particle jumps to the opposite corner of its block. A lone particle therefore flies along a diagonal of the cube, one cell in each of the three directions per step.
  • Turn: two particles on a diagonal of one side of the block (they meet at an angle) move onto the other diagonal of that side and fly on across the block: both change direction, the way two particles that meet head-on turn in Bounce Gas II.
  • Stand: a block with five or more particles, or with four that fill one side, stays as it is for that step. A particle that runs into a wall or a crowd stops for one step, and then it is in the corner that leads back: it comes back the way it came. That is the bounce.

What the rule does, and what it does not. Every sentence below was held against a run by a test of the experiment or measured by the research.

  • No two blocks give the same block, and every entry keeps the number of particles: the rule can be run backwards exactly (the table is even its own inverse), and the count of particles never changes. Back on this page computes the past; it goes back before the start too.
  • Turned or mirrored, a block does the same thing turned or mirrored: the rule has all 48 symmetries of the cube. That is also why it cannot turn two particles that meet head-on along the same diagonal: no other direction is the one to turn to, so they pass through each other.
  • In a thin gas the particles fly and meet now and then; directions that start out few get mixed by the turns and the bounces. In a thick gas about half the particles stand in any step (measured at half full), and a gas of 70 % is nearly solid.
  • A flat wall two cells thick stands still, and so does a world full of particles with a room shaped like a cube cut out of it: a gas in the room bounces off the walls and never gets out (2,000 steps measured). A box in empty space does not stand: its outer edges and corners fly off.
  • No small group stays together: whatever fits in one block has flown apart, single particle by single particle, after two steps. Nothing that fits in 2 × 2 × 2 cells, and no two or three particles in 4 × 4 × 4, stands still, comes back or flies other than as lone particles.
What the right picture shows

The graph has no rows, no columns and no layers, only nodes and arrows. Every cell is three nodes:

  • two corners: one belongs to the cell's block of even steps, one to its block of odd steps. Each is drawn towards the middle of its block. The eight corners of a block are joined along the 12 edges of a cube, with an arrow each way along every edge: the cube of that block, drawn as one line per edge;
  • a middle node, drawn beside the line between the two corners.

Three arrows belong to the cell itself, a small triangle, as on the Bounce Gas II page. The cross arrow goes from one corner to the other and leaves the corner whose block acts next: it says whose turn it is. The middle node either lies on the way from that corner to the other (the cell is empty) or points at both corners (the cell holds a particle). So a cell has 3 nodes and 9 arrows (its triangle and three arrows of each of its two cubes), never two arrows between the same two nodes in the same direction, and no arrow from a node to itself.

Why arrows both ways. On the flat page a block is a square with arrows round it one way, which looks the same after every quarter turn. A cube cannot do that: for any two corners some turn of the cube swaps them, so an arrow one way between them would come out the other way round. With an arrow each way the cube looks the same after all 48 of its symmetries, and so does the table, so a rule may find a block in any of these 48 ways and does the same thing.

A rule takes a cube whose eight cross arrows all leave it, and puts back the same cube with the eight cross arrows turned round and each middle node as the table says: 36 arrows in, 36 arrows out, on the same 24 nodes. Nothing is made, nothing is destroyed, arrows only turn. Turning the cross arrows passes the turn to the cells' other blocks: a block of the other cut can act once all eight of its cells point away from it. In each step the engine applies every rule that fits, so all blocks of one cut act together, then all of the other, without any clock outside the graph. There are 22 rules, one for each kind of block up to a turn or mirror image of the cube.
