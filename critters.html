What Critters is

Critters comes from the book Cellular Automata Machines by Tommaso Toffoli and Norman Margolus (1987). It is a block automaton (technical name: a cellular automaton with the Margolus neighbourhood): it cuts the world into squares of 2 × 2 cells, the blocks, and replaces each block as a whole by what a table of 16 entries says. On even steps the blocks start at the top left corner; on odd steps the cut moves one cell right and down (the two cuts, or phases), so every block of an odd step holds one cell of four blocks of the step before, and that is how things travel.

Critters is reversible: no two blocks become the same block, so every step can be undone exactly, and Back on this page computes the past (also before the start, where the world wraps round or has walls). It never makes or destroys a particle. And from this little, things that look alive come out: gliders that fly, groups that come back again and again, and a random blob that falls apart into gliders. The simulator js-revca by Dmitry Shintyakov shows it too; it calls the reading below "hide vacuum changes".

Why this page shows two tables, not the one in the book

The book gives one table for both cuts: a block is turned inside out (every particle gone, every empty cell filled) unless it holds exactly two particles; a block of three is also given half a turn. Played like that, an empty block becomes full, so the whole world flashes from empty to full on every step. This page never shows that.

Instead it shows every second step turned inside out. That is exactly the same automaton, only looked at differently: on even steps you see the book's world, on odd steps its opposite. Seen that way, empty space stays empty, and the rule becomes two tables that take turns, one for the even blocks and one for the odd blocks. Each of the two is its own undo: the same table on the same blocks takes a step back. Tests of the experiment hold that this world, turned inside out on every odd step, equals the book's Critters in every cell.

The table of the odd blocks is exactly Bounce Gas II, the rule of this experiment's second page. So Critters is Bounce Gas II on the odd blocks and one more table on the even blocks.

      What comes out of it. Every sentence below was held against a run by a test of the experiment.

      • The number of particles never changes (where the world wraps round or has walls).
      • A glider is four particles that fly straight, half a cell a step: after 4 steps it has the same shape, 2 cells further. Of all patterns of up to 5 particles in a box of 4 × 4 cells, in every place against the blocks, the glider is the only one that flies, and nothing flies along a diagonal (searched in the research, every one of them).
      • Two gliders that meet head-on come out of the meeting as two gliders: 8 empty columns apart they both turn by a quarter (one up, one down), 6 apart they fly on as if they had never met. Two side by side fly as one ship, at a quarter of a cell a step; a "slow ship" of eight flies at an eighth. Everything else small comes back to where it was: a lone particle after 4 steps, the glider's shape moved by one cell after 8, a row of seven after 48.
      • A random blob in empty space falls apart and sends out gliders. A world filled at random stays a random mess: the blob is the start that shows something.
      What the right picture shows

      The graph has no rows and no columns, only nodes and arrows. Every cell is three nodes: two corners (one in the square of its even block, one in the square of its odd block), drawn towards the middle of their block, and a middle node drawn beside the cell's diagonal. Three arrows belong to the cell itself, a small triangle: the cross arrow goes from one corner to the other and leaves the corner whose block acts next (it is the clock: whose turn it is), and the middle node either lies on the way from that corner to the other (the cell is empty) or points at both corners (the cell holds a particle).

      The four corners of a block are joined by four arrows, its square, and here is the one new thing: the two cuts have squares of two different shapes. An odd block has a round square, four arrows one way round, as on the Bounce Gas page. An even block has a zigzag square: its four arrows leave the top left and bottom right corners (they send) and enter the other two (they receive).

      Why two shapes. A rule can only ask for what is in the graph. If both squares looked the same, nothing could tell a rule of the even blocks from a rule of the odd blocks, and each would fit both. Adding a mark to one cut does not help, because a square with something more still fits the rules of the other cut: each kind of square needs something the other lacks. In a round square every corner has one arrow in and one out; in a zigzag square every corner has two out or two in. Neither shape is part of the other. ("Clockwise against anticlockwise" would not do: a graph has no clockwise.) So a cell still has 3 nodes and 5 arrows, as on the Bounce Gas page, no two arrows join the same two nodes, and no arrow points from a node to itself.

      A rule takes one square whose four cross arrows all leave it and puts back the same square with the four cross arrows turned round and each middle node as the table says: 16 arrows on 12 nodes, nothing made, nothing destroyed. Turning the cross arrows passes the turn to the other cut, so in each step of the engine all blocks of one cut act, then all of the other, with no clock outside the graph. There is one rule per kind of block: six for the round squares (the rules of Bounce Gas II, unchanged) and nine for the zigzag squares. A round square can be met at any of its four corners, so one rule covers a block and its quarter turns; a zigzag square tells a sending corner from a receiving one, so a lone particle at a sending corner and one at a receiving corner need a rule each.

      The 15 rules

      Each rule as the block before and after, with its square drawn over it, a sentence, and the rule in the notation of the Wolfram Physics Project: every pair {a,b} is an arrow from a to b; q0 to q3 are the corners of the square in clockwise order from the top left (on a zigzag square q0 and q2 send), p a cell's other corner and m its middle node.

      The one table is the leanest, but in its graph empty space turns full on every odd step, which is the flashing again, only hidden by the reading, and "an empty block becomes full" is a rule nobody can see in the picture. Of the ones that keep empty space empty, the two shapes of square cost nothing beyond the Bounce Gas graph, every rule is said in one sentence you can check against its picture, and with round odd squares the odd half is literally the Bounce Gas II graph, walls and open edge included. A mark inside the cell's own triangle was ruled out by reasoning: the four states of a cell (empty or full, which cut is next) use up the ways a triangle can be drawn.
