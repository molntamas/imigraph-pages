imigraph pages

imigraph imitates systems with graphs: instead of computing a world the usual way, it is written as nodes, relations between them, and rules that rewrite the relations. These pages run in the browser; nothing has to be installed.

  • Retro Life: a Game of Life game that shows its hypergraph

    Conway's Game of Life as a small game for phone, tablet and desktop, in pixel art with a Japanese touch. Three screens and six buttons.

    • MAP: alive cells are little faces. A finger or the mouse sets and unsets cells; two fingers or the wheel zoom. It opens calmly, with one glider, one block and one beacon that never meet.
    • The colours of alive cells tell how long a cell has been alive: one colour for a cell just born, one for a young cell, and one for an old cell that has been alive for 12 generations or more. So what never changes soon looks different from what moves and blinks.
    • DONUT: the map with its edges glued. The world wraps round, so glue its left and right edges and then its top and bottom, and it closes into a ring; every cell keeps its eight neighbours and the rule stays the same. The same ring is the world as a hypergraph in the manner of the Wolfram Physics Project, floating in space: every cell is a node on a ring, a faint grey rock while it is dead and a coloured light while it is alive, fainter the further away it is. Thin dark lines are the relations that say which cells are neighbours; coloured lines are the life signals, the lines along which an alive node tells its eight neighbours that it is alive. Drag to turn the ring any way you like, also to see it from the top; tap a node to set its cell. While the world plays, the ring turns slowly by itself; a switch in the settings keeps it still. Up and down there give the same cells another form: a Klein bottle, drawn two ways (as a ring whose tube is a figure of eight, and as a hand-blown bottle whose neck passes through its own wall), where what leaves on the right comes back on the left upside down (in space its tube has to pass through itself), or two donuts joined by a door, the upper and the lower half of the map. A switch moves no cell, and switching back gives the same donut.
    • ORGANISM: one organism of the world, a glider for instance, close up, in a window on the grid of the ring, laid flat. A larger screen shows a larger piece of the grid, and the picture stands still while the organism lives in it. The world plays slowly there, one generation a second. Two arrow buttons or a tap on the small map choose the organism. A row of three buttons changes what the window shows, and nothing moves when you switch:
      • Normal: the organism as it is, faces and rocks, with the life signals.
      • Numbers: a forecast. Every node carries the number of its alive neighbours; a light tile stays alive, a red tile dies, a hollow tile is born.
      • Graph: every cell opened up. Here a cell is not one node but a small graph of its own, as it could look inside when Life is played by a more general kind of system that knows only nodes, arrows between them and rules that rewrite small pieces of such a graph: a ring of eight nodes, one door for each neighbour, with an arrow each way between the doors of two neighbours. Four more arrows, the cords, lie across the ring while the cell is alive and rest along its rim while it is dead; being born and dying only swing them. A ring with its cords across cannot be drawn without crossings, a knot of the kind the Wolfram Physics Project pictures particles as; here it is the mark of life, tied and undone by the rules. An alive cell wears its face in its ring, and the lines that carry life to its neighbours have a colour of their own: count the coloured lines that arrive at a cell and you have the rule of Life.
      Minus and plus (or the wheel, or two fingers) show more of the world or look closer, from a single cell to the whole world. Close up, the window keeps one cell of the organism in its middle, and a tap on another cell goes there.
    • The plus adds one of 31 formations (gliders and spaceships, still lifes, oscillators, two glider guns, two machines that turn or double a glider, shapes that live long). The wheel holds everything else: speed, one generation back, 15 maps up to a world of 512 × 512 cells (one of them an AND gate built of glider guns, with its truth table), three looks (Yoru, the night, and Hiru, the day, with the ring in a blue sky and a sun, in pixel art; Kuroi: black and white, plain and smooth), full screen (on a tablet the game is then not left by mistake), ready views of the ring, saving, pattern text, and an explanation of what is on the screen.
    • Learn Game of Life, at the top of the settings, is a small tutorial of 16 lessons on a world of its own. First what the Game of Life is, then the rule: you keep a cell alive, let one be born, count the alive neighbours of every place, say what the next generation will be, and build a shape that stays and one that blinks. Then the game: what the colours of the cells mean, how to add a shape with the plus and load a map from the menu, and a look at the two other screens. The button to press next blinks, a solved lesson is cheered, and the arrows go back and forward through the lessons. Your world waits until you come back. The tutorial has a link of its own, to send to someone who wants to learn the rule: retro_life.html#learn.
    • Organisms: a book of the shapes of Life in the settings, a page for every formation (what it is, what it does, the shapes it goes through, and a button to try it). On the screen of an organism its name stands at the bottom when it is a known one, a glider say (on a phone held upright at the top right); a tap opens its page.
    • How the screens belong together: on the page below, 17 rewriting rules play Life on exactly this hypergraph. This game turns that round: the ordinary rule computes, which is far faster, and the hypergraph is shown as the rules would hold it at that moment. The view Graph shows a second set of 17 rules the same way: one that plays Life on a graph of arrows only.
    • Big maps (settings, part Big maps): patterns far larger than any world of the game, shown as a map only on a plane without borders and computed by HashLife, an algorithm that jumps over many generations at once. A digital clock (10,016 × 6,796 cells), by an anonymous author, and the Waterbear, a spaceship of 197,896 cells that crawls across the plane (by biggiemac42, from designs of Dave Greene, codeholic and others, as its builder tells it); and Open a file for a pattern of your own. The licences of these two patterns are not stated and are being looked into; if you are their author and want one removed, tell the owner of this site.

  • Game of graphs: the Game of Life on a Wolfram model

    Conway's Game of Life twice, side by side. On the left it is computed the ordinary way. On the right the same world is a hypergraph in the manner of the Wolfram Physics Project: every cell is a node, and 17 rewriting rules do what the rule of Life does. After every generation the page checks that both sides agree in every cell.

    • Set up a world (6 × 6 to 96 × 96 cells, ready patterns, random, or draw it), then start, pause, step, restart, and play slower or faster. The patterns include spaceships, Diehard and Acorn, the Gosper glider gun, a gun whose gliders an eater swallows, and crashes.
    • "Full window" shows a picture alone, as large as the browser window (the hypergraph fills it also on a phone held sideways; the 3D hypergraph has the button too); an X in the top right corner or Esc goes back, Start and Stop buttons at the bottom (or Space) play and pause, and Back and Forward move one generation (Back is in the button bar too); the buttons appear when you move the mouse or touch the screen.
    • Play step by step to watch how it is done: every cell sends its state to its eight neighbours, a reader collects the eight messages and counts, and the count decides.
    • A second tab shows the hypergraph in 3D without the grid, placed only by which nodes are related. The world wraps round, so it shows as a ring.
    • Click a node (or "Show an interesting cell") to see the relations of that one cell drawn the way the Wolfram project draws a hypergraph, with its eight neighbours round it. The picture follows the play, marks in red what each step made, and has its own buttons to step back through the generation; a tick box colours every cell by its distance from the chosen one.
    • Every picture of the hypergraph zooms with the mouse wheel or, on a phone, with two fingers; a zoomed-in grid moves with a drag.
    • A switch at the top leads to a Game of Life in three dimensions: a box of cells, each touching 26 others, played by Carter Bays' Life 5766 (or Life 4555, or B45/S456). Alive cells are cubes on the left; on the right the hypergraph shows every node and every relation. Both turn together, drawn on the graphics card (WebGL). Two gliders climb up through the layers. Open it in 3D.
    • The page explains every mark in the picture, how a rule gets chosen and why ten steps of the rules are one generation of Life, why the model looks the way it does, and what was found of earlier work.

    It runs Python in the browser (Pyodide), which is downloaded when the page opens: allow a few seconds for the start.

  • Bounce Gas II on a graph: a block automaton played by rewriting rules

    A second kind of cellular automaton next to the Game of Life: a block automaton. The world is cut into blocks of 2 × 2 cells and every block is replaced by what a table of 16 entries says; on the next step the cut moves by one cell. The rule is Bounce Gas II (by Tim Tyler): single particles fly diagonally, two that meet head-on turn by 90 degrees, and a particle that runs into two others stops for a step and flies back. On the right the same world is a directed graph: every cell is three nodes and five arrows, and six rewriting rules do what the table does. After every step the page checks that both sides agree in every cell.

    • Worlds from 8 × 8 to 256 × 256 cells; patterns (one particle, a head-on collision, a pair that parts, a block that flies apart, a wall that works as a mirror, a gas between walls, a gas in a diamond-shaped room) or a random soup of a chosen density.
    • Start, stop, one step, and Back: the rule can be run backwards exactly, so Back computes the past, also before the start. The number of particles never changes.
    • The page shows the table as 16 small pictures and says, sentence by sentence, what the rule does and what it does not.

    Open the page

    It runs Python in the browser (Pyodide), which is downloaded when the page opens: allow a few seconds for the start.

  • Bounce Gas 3D on a graph: a block automaton in three dimensions played by rewriting rules

    The block automaton of the page above, in three dimensions. The world is a box that wraps round in every direction, cut into blocks of 2 × 2 × 2 cells; every block is replaced by what a table of 256 entries says, and on the next step the cut moves by one cell in all three directions. No ready-made rule of this kind was found, so the rule, Bounce Gas 3D, is this project's own, made after Bounce Gas II: single particles fly along the diagonals of the cube, two that meet at an angle turn, and a crowded block stands for a step, so particles bounce off walls and crowds. On the left the particles fly as small cubes in a box; on the right the same world is a directed graph: every cell is three nodes and nine arrows, and 22 rewriting rules do what the table does. On small worlds the page checks after every step that both sides agree in every cell.

    • Worlds from 4 × 4 × 4 to 32 × 32 × 32 cells; starts (one particle, two that meet at an angle, two that meet head-on, a full block that bursts, a wall that works as a mirror, a gas in a room cut out of a full world) or a random gas of a chosen density.
    • Start, stop, one step, and Back: the rule can be run backwards exactly. The number of particles never changes.
    • Drag to turn both pictures together; nothing moves by itself. Drawn on the graphics card (WebGL), with a plainer drawing where the browser has none.

  • Critters on a graph: a block automaton with gliders played by rewriting rules

    Critters is a block automaton of Tommaso Toffoli and Norman Margolus: the world is cut into blocks of 2 × 2 cells, every block is replaced by what a table says, and on the next step the cut moves by one cell. In its book form the whole world flashes on every step; the page shows the same automaton without the flashing, as two tables that take turns (the second one is Bounce Gas II, the rule of the page above). Nothing is born and nothing dies, and four particles together fly as a glider. On the left the cells; on the right the same world as a directed graph: every cell is three nodes and five arrows, the squares of the two cuts have two different shapes, and 15 rewriting rules do what the tables do (20 with walls or an open edge). On small worlds the page checks after every step that both sides agree in every cell.

    • Worlds from 8 × 8 to 256 × 256 cells that wrap round, have walls, or have an open edge where particles leave; starts with gliders, or a random soup of a chosen density.
    • Start, stop, one step, and Back: the rule can be run backwards exactly.
    • The page shows both tables, the rules with pictures of their blocks, and the other graphs that were tried.

  • Bounce Gas II, its graph, and Lean: a proof that the graph plays the automaton

    The pages above check, step by step and on the world you set up, that a graph rewritten by rules does what a cellular automaton does. This page is about saying it once and for all. The automaton Bounce Gas II and the directed graph that plays it with six rewriting rules are written again in Lean, a programming language in which a statement and its proof can be written down and a machine checks the proof. Proved there, for worlds of every even size: whatever rule is applied wherever, in whatever order, every cell of the graph holds exactly what the automaton has in that cell.

    • Try it: a small world and its graph side by side. A ring marks every block that may act; you make the events yourself, one block at a time, in any order. A number in each cell says how many events it has been through, and four lines say, for the world on your screen, what was proved for all worlds.
    • The table of the rule and the six rewriting rules, drawn next to the Lean text that says the same; one proof read line by line; the theorem and how its proof goes.
    • What is proved (146 statements, each checked by Lean), what each rests on, and what is not proved.
    • What Lean is, in a few sentences, and where it is used in physics.

    Open the page

    It starts at once and loads nothing: the part to play with runs on the table that Lean wrote out.

  • Universes that grow their own space: how space and movement come out of rewriting rules

    The pages above put the space in by hand: a grid of cells, written into the start graph. In the Wolfram Physics Project a single rewriting rule starts from two relations and grows its own space. This page shows the results of a research pass on which of these universes look like physics, how they can be drawn so that the picture stands still, and what is known about things that move in them. Its plain finding: no rule is known, in the project's sources or in our measurements, that makes both a space and something lasting that moves through it.

    • Space grows: a rule that grows a flat mesh of triangles, drawn without a single crossing, step by step; three ways of placing the nodes to compare.
    • A marker moves through it: put in by hand, one hop per change, wandering when the order of changes is random; you can move it yourself by tapping a node.
    • Curved and crumpled universes in 3D, turned only by dragging; tap a node to colour the others by their number of hops from it.
    • World lines: the project's own example of a particle, a kink on a ring, with time as height; beside each scene the graph of which change needed which.
    • What here is like physics and what is not, and what of another AI's claims about particles could be found in the sources.
