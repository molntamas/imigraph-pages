imigraph pages

imigraph imitates systems with graphs: instead of computing a world the usual way, it is written as nodes, relations between them, and rules that rewrite the relations. These pages run in the browser; nothing has to be installed.

  • Retro Life: a Game of Life game that shows its hypergraph

    Conway's Game of Life as a small game for phone, tablet and desktop, in pixel art with a Japanese touch. Three screens and six buttons.

    • MAP: alive cells are little faces. A finger or the mouse sets and unsets cells; two fingers or the wheel zoom. It opens calmly, with one glider, one block and one beacon that never meet.
    • The colours of alive cells tell how long a cell has been alive: one colour for a cell just born, one for a young cell, and one for an old cell that has been alive for 12 generations or more. So what never changes soon looks different from what moves and blinks.
    • DONUT: the map with its edges glued. The world wraps round, so glue its left and right edges and then its top and bottom, and it closes into a ring; every cell keeps its eight neighbours and the rule stays the same. The same ring is the world as a hypergraph in the manner of the Wolfram Physics Project, floating in space: every cell is a node on a ring, a faint grey rock while it is dead and a coloured light while it is alive, fainter the further away it is. Thin dark lines are the relations that say which cells are neighbours; coloured lines are the life signals, the lines along which an alive node tells its eight neighbours that it is alive. Drag to turn the ring any way you like, also to see it from the top; tap a node to set its cell. While the world plays, the ring turns slowly by itself; a switch in the settings keeps it still.
    • ORGANISM: one organism of the world, a glider for instance, close up, in a window on the grid of the ring, laid flat. A larger screen shows a larger piece of the grid, and the picture stands still while the organism lives in it. The world plays slowly there, one generation a second. Two arrow buttons or a tap on the small map choose the organism. A row of three buttons changes what the window shows, and nothing moves when you switch:
      • Normal: the organism as it is, faces and rocks, with the life signals.
      • Numbers: a forecast. Every node carries the number of its alive neighbours; a light tile stays alive, a red tile dies, a hollow tile is born.
      • Graph: every cell opened up. Here a cell is not one node but a small graph of its own, as it could look inside when Life is played by a more general kind of system that knows only nodes, arrows between them and rules that rewrite small pieces of such a graph: a ring of eight nodes, one door for each neighbour, with an arrow each way between the doors of two neighbours. Four more arrows, the cords, lie across the ring while the cell is alive and rest along its rim while it is dead; being born and dying only swing them. A ring with its cords across cannot be drawn without crossings, a knot of the kind the Wolfram Physics Project pictures particles as; here it is the mark of life, tied and undone by the rules. An alive cell wears its face in its ring, and the lines that carry life to its neighbours have a colour of their own: count the coloured lines that arrive at a cell and you have the rule of Life.
      Minus and plus (or the wheel, or two fingers) show more of the world or look closer, from a single cell to the whole world. Close up, the window keeps one cell of the organism in its middle, and a tap on another cell goes there.
    • The plus adds one of 29 formations (gliders and spaceships, still lifes, oscillators, two glider guns, shapes that live long). The wheel holds everything else: speed, one generation back, 14 maps up to a world of 512 × 512 cells, four looks (three in pixel art, and Kuroi: black and white, plain and smooth), ready views of the ring, saving, pattern text, and an explanation of what is on the screen.
    • Learn Game of Life, at the top of the settings, is a small tutorial: nine lessons on a map of their own, in which you keep a cell alive, let one be born, say what the next generation will be, and build a shape that stays and one that blinks. Your world waits until you come back.
    • How the screens belong together: on the page below, 17 rewriting rules play Life on exactly this hypergraph. This game turns that round: the ordinary rule computes, which is far faster, and the hypergraph is shown as the rules would hold it at that moment. The view Graph shows a second set of 17 rules the same way: one that plays Life on a graph of arrows only.

    Open the game

    One small file that loads nothing else: it starts at once and, once open, needs no network.

    Kept versions: v1 of the game, as it first stood on 21 November 2026 (two screens; without the life signals and the screen of an organism, with solid rocks and a ring that turned only part of the way). It stays as it is; the link above is always the latest version.

  • Game of graphs: the Game of Life on a Wolfram model

    Conway's Game of Life twice, side by side. On the left it is computed the ordinary way. On the right the same world is a hypergraph in the manner of the Wolfram Physics Project: every cell is a node, and 17 rewriting rules do what the rule of Life does. After every generation the page checks that both sides agree in every cell.

    • Set up a world (6 × 6 to 96 × 96 cells, ready patterns, random, or draw it), then start, pause, step, restart, and play slower or faster. The patterns include spaceships, Diehard and Acorn, the Gosper glider gun, a gun whose gliders an eater swallows, and crashes.
    • "Full window" shows a picture alone, as large as the browser window (the hypergraph fills it also on a phone held sideways; the 3D hypergraph has the button too); an X in the top right corner or Esc goes back, Start and Stop buttons at the bottom (or Space) play and pause, and Back and Forward move one generation (Back is in the button bar too); the buttons appear when you move the mouse or touch the screen.
    • Play step by step to watch how it is done: every cell sends its state to its eight neighbours, a reader collects the eight messages and counts, and the count decides.
    • A second tab shows the hypergraph in 3D without the grid, placed only by which nodes are related. The world wraps round, so it shows as a ring.
    • Click a node (or "Show an interesting cell") to see the relations of that one cell drawn the way the Wolfram project draws a hypergraph, with its eight neighbours round it. The picture follows the play, marks in red what each step made, and has its own buttons to step back through the generation; a tick box colours every cell by its distance from the chosen one.
    • Every picture of the hypergraph zooms with the mouse wheel or, on a phone, with two fingers; a zoomed-in grid moves with a drag.
    • A switch at the top leads to a Game of Life in three dimensions: a box of cells, each touching 26 others, played by Carter Bays' Life 5766 (or Life 4555, or B45/S456). Alive cells are cubes on the left; on the right the hypergraph shows every node and every relation. Both turn together, drawn on the graphics card (WebGL). Two gliders climb up through the layers. Open it in 3D.
    • The page explains every mark in the picture, how a rule gets chosen and why ten steps of the rules are one generation of Life, why the model looks the way it does, and what was found of earlier work.

    Open the page

    It runs Python in the browser (Pyodide), which is downloaded when the page opens: allow a few seconds for the start.
