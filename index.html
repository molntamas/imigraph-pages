imigraph pages

imigraph imitates systems with graphs: instead of computing a world the usual way, it is written as nodes, relations between them, and rules that rewrite the relations. These pages run in the browser; nothing has to be installed.

  • Retro Life: a Game of Life game that shows its hypergraph

    Conway's Game of Life as a small game for phone, tablet and desktop, in pixel art with a Japanese touch. Two screens and six buttons.

    • MAP: alive cells are little faces. A finger or the mouse sets and unsets cells; two fingers or the wheel zoom. It opens calmly, with one glider, one block and one beacon that never meet.
    • DONUT: the same world as a hypergraph in the manner of the Wolfram Physics Project, floating in space: every cell is a node on a ring, a grey rock while it is dead and a coloured light while it is alive, with thin lines for the relations that say which cells are neighbours. Drag to turn it, tap a node to set its cell.
    • The plus adds one of 29 formations (gliders and spaceships, still lifes, oscillators, two glider guns, shapes that live long). The wheel holds everything else: speed, one generation back, 14 maps up to a world of 512 × 512 cells, three looks, saving, pattern text.
    • How the two screens belong together: on the page below, 17 rewriting rules play Life on exactly this hypergraph. This game turns that round: the ordinary rule computes, which is far faster, and the hypergraph is shown as the rules would hold it at that moment.

    Open the game

    One small file that loads nothing else: it starts at once and, once open, needs no network.

    Kept versions: v1 of the game, as it was on 21 November 2026. It stays as it is; the link above is always the latest version.

  • Game of graphs: the Game of Life on a Wolfram model

    Conway's Game of Life twice, side by side. On the left it is computed the ordinary way. On the right the same world is a hypergraph in the manner of the Wolfram Physics Project: every cell is a node, and 17 rewriting rules do what the rule of Life does. After every generation the page checks that both sides agree in every cell.

    • Set up a world (6 × 6 to 96 × 96 cells, ready patterns, random, or draw it), then start, pause, step, restart, and play slower or faster. The patterns include spaceships, Diehard and Acorn, the Gosper glider gun, a gun whose gliders an eater swallows, and crashes.
    • "Full window" shows a picture alone, as large as the browser window (the hypergraph fills it also on a phone held sideways; the 3D hypergraph has the button too); an X in the top right corner or Esc goes back, Start and Stop buttons at the bottom (or Space) play and pause, and Back and Forward move one generation (Back is in the button bar too); the buttons appear when you move the mouse or touch the screen.
    • Play step by step to watch how it is done: every cell sends its state to its eight neighbours, a reader collects the eight messages and counts, and the count decides.
    • A second tab shows the hypergraph in 3D without the grid, placed only by which nodes are related. The world wraps round, so it shows as a ring.
    • Click a node (or "Show an interesting cell") to see the relations of that one cell drawn the way the Wolfram project draws a hypergraph, with its eight neighbours round it. The picture follows the play, marks in red what each step made, and has its own buttons to step back through the generation; a tick box colours every cell by its distance from the chosen one.
    • Every picture of the hypergraph zooms with the mouse wheel or, on a phone, with two fingers; a zoomed-in grid moves with a drag.
    • A switch at the top leads to a Game of Life in three dimensions: a box of cells, each touching 26 others, played by Carter Bays' Life 5766 (or Life 4555, or B45/S456). Alive cells are cubes on the left; on the right the hypergraph shows every node and every relation. Both turn together, drawn on the graphics card (WebGL). Two gliders climb up through the layers. Open it in 3D.
    • The page explains every mark in the picture, how a rule gets chosen and why ten steps of the rules are one generation of Life, why the model looks the way it does, and what was found of earlier work.

    Open the page

    It runs Python in the browser (Pyodide), which is downloaded when the page opens: allow a few seconds for the start.
