What a block automaton is

An ordinary cellular automaton, like Conway's Game of Life, looks at every cell and its neighbours and decides the cell's next state. A block automaton (technical name: a cellular automaton with the Margolus neighbourhood, after Norman Margolus) works on blocks instead: it cuts the world into squares of 2 × 2 cells and replaces each square as a whole. A block of four cells can be in 16 states, so the whole rule is a table of 16 entries.

If the cut stayed the same, nothing could ever leave its block. So the cut moves: on even steps the blocks start at the top left corner, on odd steps one cell further right and down (the two partitions, or phases). Every block of an odd step holds one cell of each of four blocks of the even step before, and that is how things travel. At the right and bottom border the odd blocks take their other cells from the left and top border: the world wraps round, like the Life page.

The table: what each block becomes

All 16 blocks, before and after one step. A block is read as a number: top left 1, top right 2, bottom left 4, bottom right 8 (the order of the simulator js-revca, read in its source). The list of Bounce Gas II is 0, 8, 4, 12, 2, 10, 9, 7, 1, 6, 5, 11, 3, 13, 14, 15.

What the table does, and what it does not. Every sentence below was held against a run by a test of the experiment.

  • No two blocks give the same block, and every entry keeps the number of particles: the rule can be run backwards exactly (the table is even its own inverse), and the count of particles never changes. Back on this page computes the past; it goes back before the start too.
  • Turned or mirrored, a block does the same thing turned or mirrored (the rule has the eight symmetries of a square).
  • One particle jumps to the opposite corner of its block. In the next step it is in the corner of the moved block that leads on the same way, so it flies diagonally, one cell per step, and on a world of n × n cells it is home after n steps.
  • Two side by side move one row (or column) together, and then part, each on its own diagonal. A description of this rule found on the web says pairs "travel orthogonally"; they do not.
  • Two meeting head-on on a diagonal turn onto the other diagonal and fly apart: a turn by 90 degrees.
  • Three or four in a block stay as they are for that step. The same description says they form "stationary walls"; a small group does not: in the next step its cells are alone in their blocks and fly off. A particle that stands still for one step comes back the way it came: that is the bounce.
  • What does stand still: walls along the diagonals, three cells thick, and a world full of particles around a room shaped like a diamond (a square on its corner). Particles bounce off them and never get through (patterns A wall as a mirror, Gas between walls, A gas in a diamond room). No pattern that fits in 4 × 4 cells stands still; all of them fly apart.
What the right picture shows

The graph has no rows and no columns, only nodes and arrows. Every cell is three nodes:

  • two corners: one belongs to the cell's block of even steps, one to its block of odd steps. The four corners of a block are joined by four arrows in a ring, clockwise: the square of that block. Each corner is drawn towards the middle of its block, so the squares of even and odd blocks alternate across the picture;
  • a middle node, drawn beside the cell's diagonal.

Three arrows belong to the cell itself, a small triangle. The cross arrow goes from one corner to the other and leaves the corner whose block acts next: it says whose turn it is. The middle node either lies on the way from that corner to the other (the cell is empty) or points at both corners (the cell holds a particle). So a cell has 3 nodes and 5 arrows (its triangle and one arrow of each of its two squares), no two arrows join the same two nodes, and no arrow points from a node to itself.

A rule takes a square whose four cross arrows all leave it, and puts back the same square with the four cross arrows turned round and each middle node as the table says. It uses 16 arrows and gives back 16 arrows on the same 12 nodes: nothing is made, nothing is destroyed, arrows only turn. Turning the cross arrows passes the turn to the cells' other blocks: a block of the other partition can act once all four of its cells point away from it, which happens when the four blocks it overlaps have acted. In each step the engine applies every rule that fits, so all blocks of one partition act together, then all of the other: the two partitions take turns without any clock outside the graph. There are six rules, one for each kind of block up to a quarter turn (empty, one, two side by side, two across, three, four); a square has no first corner, and the table is the same turned, so that is enough.

The 6 rules

In the notation of the Wolfram Physics Project: every pair {a,b} is an arrow from a to b; q0 to q3 are the corners of the square in clockwise order, p a cell's other corner and m its middle node.
